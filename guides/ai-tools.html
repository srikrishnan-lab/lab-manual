AI Tools in Research

This page covers how we use language models and related tools in the group’s research, and how to decide when a use isn’t appropriate.

ImportantWhat Are These Tools For?

Use these tools to support your judgment, not to replace it.

You are here to become a researcher. That means learning to read a hard paper, find your own bugs, notice when a result looks too clean, and form your own view before someone tells you theirs. Each of these is a skill, and you build skills through practice, including the parts that are slow and uncomfortable.

If a tool does that work for you, you’ll finish this week’s task sooner, but you won’t have built the skill. You’ll need it later, when the tool can’t help or when you have to check what it gave you.

Cognitive Offloading

Our concern on this page is with what these tools do to your skills, which is a separate question from whether their output is accurate. Because the tools are useful, it’s easy to hand them work without noticing that you’ve stopped practicing it.

This problem was described long before language models. Bainbridge’s “Ironies of Automation”1 looked at automated process control and flight decks, where operators are left to monitor the automation and take over when something goes wrong. The paper argues that the operator is left with the tasks the designers couldn’t automate, including the take-over, while automation removes the practice those tasks need: manual skills “deteriorate when they are not used,” and the knowledge needed to handle an unusual situation “develops only through use and feedback about its effectiveness.”

1 Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of automation. Automatica, 19(6), 775–779. https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8.

2 Liu, G., Christian, B., Dumbalska, T., Bakker, M. A., & Dubey, R. (2026). AI Assistance Reduces Persistence and Hurts Independent Performance. Conference on Language Modeling (COLM 2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04721.

3 Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., et al. (2025). Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Task. Preprint, under review. https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872.

Two recent studies find related effects with AI assistants. In a set of randomized experiments with 1,222 online participants solving fraction problems and reading-comprehension questions, people who had AI help did better while they had it, then solved fewer problems and skipped more of them once the help was removed, compared with people who never had it2. These effects appeared after sessions of only 10–15 minutes. In an EEG study of 54 people writing essays over several months, participants who used an LLM showed the weakest neural connectivity of three groups (LLM, search engine, no tools). In a final session, nine of the LLM users wrote without it; they showed weaker connectivity, and 7 of the 9 could not quote a sentence from the essay they had just written, against 1 of the 9 participants who switched the other way3.

These studies are short or small (the crossover result in the essay study rests on 18 people), and the essay study has not been peer reviewed yet, so they do not tell us how large the effect is over years of research. They are consistent with Bainbridge’s mechanism, though, and that mechanism is the reason for the guidance on this page.

Deciding Whether a Use Is Reasonable

For any use you’re unsure about, ask: could I check this output myself, with the skills I have now?

If you could, the tool is saving you time on work you already know how to do, and you’re still responsible for the result. If you could not, and you would be accepting the output because you cannot evaluate it, then the tool is doing the judgment for you, which is what we want to avoid.

The answer depends on what you know, so the same use can be fine for one person and not for another. Having a model draft boilerplate in a language you know well is fine. Having it write an analysis using a method you don’t understand isn’t, even if the code runs and the numbers look reasonable.

Examples

These uses are reasonable, as long as you check the result:

  • explaining an unfamiliar error message, library, or equation, as a starting point that you then verify;
  • boilerplate you could have written yourself (plotting scaffolds, argument parsing, file input and output);
  • talking through a design decision before you commit to it;
  • tightening prose you have already written;
  • critiquing your own draft before you send it to anyone.

These uses aren’t:

  • anything that goes to someone else as your own judgment, above all a referee report (several journals and funders prohibit this; see Peer Review below);
  • generating citations, because models produce references that look correct and do not exist, and one invented citation discredits everything else you wrote;
  • writing the parts of a paper that are supposed to be your contribution: the hypothesis, the framing, the interpretation, and the decision about what a result means;
  • an analysis using a method you cannot evaluate.
WarningNever Cite What You Have Not Read

A model will give you an author, a year, a journal, and a DOI that look exactly right for a paper that does not exist, or it will describe a real paper inaccurately.

Check every reference against the paper itself. A search snippet isn’t enough, and neither is the abstract when you’re citing what the paper found. This applies to citations a model suggested and equally to ones you half-remember.

The Group’s Skills

The group maintains claude-research-skills (private to the group), a set of Claude Agent Skills built around our workflow: adversarial reading, paper planning, literature review, figure design, code review, and revision response.

The skills review and help plan work that you’re doing; they don’t write it for you. The repository’s design notes put it this way for the paper-planning skill:

What comes out of this conversation is not your contract. It is a draft, with placeholders, the assistant’s compressions of what you said, and suggestions marked as suggestions. The hypothesis, the scope decision, and the framing have to be yours. […] A contract you did not write is a contract you will not maintain, and its entire value is that it records what you predicted before you knew the answer.

The other skills follow the same rule. Referee simulation only works on your own papers, the revision-response skill evaluates a reply you have written instead of writing one, author lists are never filled in by inference, and the literature skill will not name a paper it has not found in a search.

Installation instructions are in the repository README.

Reproducibility

Our reproducibility commitments apply to code a model helped write in the same way as to any other code:

  • You are responsible for all the code in your analysis, including code you did not write yourself. Generated code usually runs, so the syntax errors that would normally warn you something is off are missing, while logic errors can remain.
  • Test it the way you would test any code. Positive and negative controls are the relevant checks: does it recover known values, and does it find nothing in noise?
  • Be most careful where the numbers are hardest to check by eye (uncertainty propagation, tail estimates), since a plausible wrong answer there will not stand out.
  • Internal code review before submission is already required. Tell the reviewer which parts were generated.

Journal and Funder Disclosure

AGU, Elsevier, Springer Nature, PNAS and AMS all state that an AI tool cannot be listed as an author. AGU, Elsevier, Springer Nature and AMS give the same reason: authorship requires accountability, which a tool cannot take. Copernicus goes further and says generative AI must not be used for text or interpretation at all. Our own authorship criteria already rule out AI authorship, since they require both a contribution to the research and participation in drafting and revising the paper.

Beyond authorship, most current policies require:

  • disclosure of substantial AI use, usually in the Methods or an equivalent section (grammar and spelling checks generally do not need declaring);
  • full author responsibility for everything in the manuscript, including generated parts;
  • separate, stricter rules for peer review (see below).

Rules for figures differ the most between publishers: some ban AI-generated images and others allow them with disclosure. Check before you make one.

These policies change faster than this page will. The table below was checked in September 2026, so treat it as a starting point and confirm against the journal’s current author guidelines when you submit. Add the disclosure statement to the paper checklist for that submission.

Venue Policy
AGU Our main venue. No AI authors. Disclose the tool and how it was used in the Methods. LLMs may not be used to review a manuscript.
Elsevier No AI authors. A separate declaration section before the references. Different rules for different kinds of images.
Copernicus / EGU Language editing does not need to be declared; generative use does. Allows AI-generated illustrations if the tool is cited.
Springer Nature No AI authors. Document use in the Methods. AI-generated images are not permitted.
PNAS No AI authors. Disclose in the Methods or Acknowledgments.
AMS No AI authors. Disclose the tool and how it was used. Reviewers may not use AI tools.

NSF

NSF has two separate policies that are easy to confuse:

  • For proposal preparation, disclosure is encouraged but not required. NSF asks proposers to say in the project description whether and how they used generative AI (December 2023 notice).
  • For research misconduct, PAPPG 24-1 Supplement 1 (effective December 2025) extends the definition to fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism committed “through the use or assistance of other persons, entities, or tools, including artificial intelligence (AI)-based tools.”

The second policy does not add a disclosure requirement, but it does mean a fabricated citation in a proposal is misconduct whether or not a model produced it.

Peer Review and Confidentiality

ImportantKeep Manuscripts Under Review Out of AI Tools

A manuscript sent to you for review is confidential, and uploading it to an external service breaks that confidentiality regardless of what the service does with it. AGU, Elsevier, Springer Nature and AMS all tell reviewers not to upload manuscripts to AI tools, and NSF prohibits reviewers from uploading proposal content to tools it has not approved.

AMS gives a second reason: an AI system cannot explain the reasoning behind its evaluation, so the exchange between authors and reviewers that a review is meant to start cannot happen.

If you’re asked to review and would like help, please ask Vivek. Reviewing is a skill like the others on this page, and you build it by doing it.

Applicants

People applying to join the group have their own guidance on the graduate, postdoc, and undergraduate pages.

An application is the only evidence we have of what a specific person is interested in and why, so we ask applicants to write it themselves. The guided paper evaluation for graduate applicants allows AI tools, because we discuss it with the applicant afterward.

Keeping This Page Current

The tools, the policies, and the evidence are all changing. If something here is out of date, please fix it, and if you find a use that has helped your work and isn’t covered here, bring it to lab meeting. The group is still working out how to use these tools well.
