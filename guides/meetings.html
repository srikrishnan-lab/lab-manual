Meetings

The group has two standing meetings, both on Monday afternoons in Riley-Robb 319, plus one-on-ones with Vivek. This page covers what each is for and what to bring.

NoteWhere Are the Schedules?

The lab meeting schedule and each meeting’s agenda are in the labmeetings repository (private to the group). The journal club schedule and paper sign-ups are in the journal-club repository.

Both are plain Markdown tables that you edit with a pull request. Please ask Vivek for access to labmeetings if you don’t have it.

Lab Meeting

Lab meeting is on Mondays from 1:00 to 2:30pm in Riley-Robb 319. You can also attend remotely; the Zoom link is in the labmeetings repository instead of here, since this manual is public.

Lab meeting is the group’s main shared working session. A typical slot is one person presenting, and a presentation can be any of the following (the most useful ones are often the least polished):

  • Work in progress, including work that is not going well
  • A practice talk before a conference or an exam
  • A figure you are not sure about
  • A method you are trying to understand, taught to the rest of us
  • A problem you are stuck on

Asking for help when you’re stuck is an explicit expectation of everyone in the group, and lab meeting is the main place to do it. Having four people who know different things look at the same problem for an hour is often a better use of the time than a polished update.

Preparing

Add your item to the current meeting’s agenda file in labmeetings before the meeting. How much you prepare depends on the slot: a practice talk needs to be ready, while a problem you’re stuck on only needs a clear statement of the problem and what you’ve already tried.

You don’t need slides. A figure on the screen and a conversation often work better.

Attending

Attendance is expected, in person or remotely, as part of maintaining a presence in the group. If you can’t make it, please say so in Slack beforehand.

Come having thought about other people’s work as well as your own. The group’s internal peer review and code review before submission depend on people knowing enough about each other’s projects to be useful readers, and lab meeting is where most of that knowledge comes from.

Journal Club

Journal club is on Mondays from 2:30 to 3:30pm in Riley-Robb 319, directly after lab meeting.

One person presents one paper each week. The presenter picks the paper, which doesn’t have to be new or something you already understand. Papers you found hard, or that you disagree with, often lead to a better discussion than papers you can summarize cleanly.

The reading list is a reasonable source of candidates, particularly the foundational papers everyone in the group is expected to have read.

Claiming a Slot

Open a pull request against the schedule table in journal-club adding your name and the paper (with a link) to an open date. Doing this a week or two ahead gives people time to read it.

Presenting

Read the paper closely and skim its main references. You aren’t expected to defend the paper or to have resolved every question about it. Your job is to help everyone else understand it quickly and then lead a discussion: fifteen or twenty minutes of setup and forty minutes of discussion is a better split than the reverse.

It is completely acceptable, and often the most interesting outcome, to present a paper and conclude that you do not believe its central claim.

One-on-Ones with Vivek

Weekly, scheduled for an hour, arranged at the start of each semester around everyone’s teaching and coursework.

An hour is what gets reserved, not what has to be used. Plenty of weeks need twenty minutes, and ending early is a perfectly good outcome: the hour exists so that the weeks which do need it are not squeezed. The purpose is to update each other and to talk through anything that is proving difficult, not to fill the time.

These meetings are yours. Vivek will not arrive with an agenda unless something specific needs discussing, so bring one (even a rough one). Useful things to bring:

  • Where things stand since last time, and anything you are stuck on
  • A decision you need input on
  • Results you want a second opinion about, before they harden into a paper
  • Coursework, committee, exam, and deadline logistics
  • Career questions, which are in scope and do not need a special occasion
ImportantDo I Need to Make Progress Every Week?

Do not treat the meeting as a weekly accounting of output. Expecting substantial progress every single week is unrealistic, and measuring yourself that way is a reliable route to feeling like you are failing at a job you are doing fine.

Research does not arrive on a seven-day cycle. A week can go to a bug that turns out to be a one-character fix, to a method that does not work, to reading that does not resolve into anything yet, or to coursework. None of that is wasted: it is what the work consists of, and it is often where the understanding comes from.

So the honest report is the useful one. “This didn’t work and I’m not sure why” is a better agenda item than a polished summary, because it is the kind of thing the meeting can actually help with. The meeting is more valuable when it is accurate than when it is impressive.

If something urgent comes up between meetings, don’t wait for the next one. PI availability explains how to reach Vivek.

Other Regular Gatherings

The group shares a third-floor Riley-Robb Slack channel with the Anderson group, used to organize events and reading across both groups. See Group Workflow for how we use Slack generally.

Conferences are covered in the graduate admissions FAQ: usually AGU and one smaller meeting each year.
